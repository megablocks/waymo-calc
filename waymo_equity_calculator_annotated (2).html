return & risk assumptions

Volatility is the standard deviation of annual returns — it measures how widely outcomes are dispersed around the expected return, not the peak-to-trough swing in a single year.

With 45% volatility and a 22% expected return, roughly two-thirds of yearly outcomes will land between -23% and +67% (±1 standard deviation). About 95% land between -68% and +112% (±2σ). The tails go further.

The S&P 500's long-run volatility is ~15–18%. Waymo at 45% reflects its pre-IPO status, capital intensity, geopolitical exposure, and concentrated technology risk — comparable to early-stage public AV companies like Tesla (~50–60% historically).

kelly criterion — optimal allocation

Kelly tells you the theoretically optimal fraction of your portfolio to allocate to an asset, given your edge (excess return) and the variance of outcomes. Bet too little and you leave compounding on the table; bet too much and volatility destroys you.

Kelly % = (expected return − risk-free rate) / volatility²

The key insight: Kelly penalizes volatility quadratically. Doubling volatility cuts the optimal allocation by 4×. This is why high-volatility assets — even ones with strong expected returns — deserve smaller allocations than intuition suggests.

If your current allocation is below Kelly optimal, the math says hold (or buy more). If it's above, you're over-concentrated relative to your edge and selling makes sense. In practice, most people use "half Kelly" to account for model uncertainty.

sharpe ratios

Sharpe measures risk-adjusted return — how much excess return you earn per unit of volatility you endure. It lets you compare assets with different return and risk profiles on a level playing field.

Sharpe = (expected return − risk-free rate) / volatility

The risk-free rate subtraction is critical: it removes the return you could earn for free in Treasuries. What's left is your reward for taking risk. Dividing by volatility normalizes it.

Rough benchmarks: below 0.5 is mediocre, 0.5–1.0 is decent, above 1.0 is excellent. If Waymo's Sharpe is significantly higher than the market's, concentration is justified on a risk-adjusted basis. If they're close, you're bearing concentration risk for marginal edge.
